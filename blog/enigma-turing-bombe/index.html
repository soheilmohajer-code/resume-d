Enigma: The Machine, the Cipher, and the Machine That Broke It

How the Enigma machine worked, why 158 quintillion settings felt unbreakable, how Alan Turing's Bombe cracked it — and how that race gave birth to modern computing and modern cryptography.

DATE: Oct 30, 2026READ: ~6 MINAUTHOR: SOHEIL MOHAJER

Every computer engineer should fall in love with one origin story, and mine is this one: a war won not only by soldiers, but by mathematicians attacking a machine — and in doing so, accidentally inventing modern computing. The Enigma story has everything: a cipher that looked mathematically invincible, a fatal design flaw, Polish heroes nobody mentioned for decades, and Alan Turing building a machine to fight another machine.

TL;DR — Enigma was a German cipher machine with ~159 quintillion daily settings. Its one mathematical flaw (a letter never encrypts to itself) plus predictable operator habits let Turing's electro-mechanical Bombe machines eliminate settings by the millions — and the decoded "Ultra" intelligence is estimated to have shortened WWII by two years.

Cryptography before Enigma: a 2,000-year arms race

Cryptography — hiding the meaning of a message — is older than the alphabet itself. Two ideas dominated for two thousand years:

  • Substitution: shift or scramble letters. Julius Caesar shifted each letter by 3: ATTACK → DWWDFN.
  • Transposition: keep the letters, shuffle their order.

The counter-attack was frequency analysis, perfected in the 9th century by Al-Kindi in Baghdad: in any language some letters appear more often, so a substitution cipher leaks its secrets through statistics. The arms race then produced the Vigenère cipher (16th century), which used a keyword to shift each letter differently — "le chiffre indéchiffrable", unbreakable, until Charles Babbage and Kasiski cracked it too in the 19th century.

The lesson that shapes everything after: a cipher's security cannot rest on secrecy of the method — attackers will know the method. (This became Kerckhoffs's principle, 1883: assume the enemy knows the system; security must live in the key alone.)

The Enigma machine

In 1918, German engineer Arthur Scherbius patented a machine that mechanized substitution so fast and so complex that frequency analysis would drown: Enigma. The German military adopted and hardened it through the 1920s and 30s.

How it worked

Imagine a typewriter wired to an electrical maze:

  1. Keyboard → rotors. Pressing a key sends current through three rotating wheels (rotors), each rewiring the alphabet differently.
  2. The reflector. Current exits the third rotor into a fixed reflector that sends it back through the rotors along a different path, lighting up one lamp — that letter is your ciphertext.
  3. The plugboard. Before and after the rotors, pairs of letters could be swapped with cables — adding enormous extra complexity.
  4. Rotation. After every keypress the right rotor steps (like an odometer), so the next letter is encrypted with a completely different wiring. AAAAA almost never comes out as BBBBB.

Each operator set the machine each morning with a daily key: which rotors (from a box of 3-out-of-5), their starting positions, ring settings, and up to 10 plugboard pairs. The result:

snippet.txt
158,962,555,217,826,360,000  ≈ 1.59 × 10²⁰ possible settings

Fifteen-eight quintillion. Try one setting per second, and you'd need more time than the age of the universe. The Germans were, reasonably, convinced it was unbreakable.

The fatal flaws

Enigma had three weaknesses — none obvious, all fatal:

  1. A letter can never encrypt to itself. The reflector guaranteed it. Sounds harmless — it's a mathematical fingerprint.
  2. Humans are predictable. Germans sent predictable messages at predictable times: weather reports (WETTER), routine reports, and formal phrases like "Heil Hitler" — so codebreakers knew pieces of the plaintext. Such guessed fragments are called cribs.
  3. The daily key wasn't the whole secret. Early procedures reused message keys in structured ways, leaking patterns the mathematicians could exploit.

The codebreakers

The first break wasn't British. In 1932, Polish mathematician Marian Rejewski — using reconstructive mathematics and intelligence from French contacts — reconstructed Enigma's internal wiring from pure paper analysis. With Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski, he built tooling including a machine called the bomba. When Germany added two more rotors in 1938, the Polish methods expired — so in July 1939, at a secret meeting near Warsaw, the Poles handed everything to British and French intelligence. Five weeks later, WWII began.

Bletchley Park, England — a country estate stuffed with crossword-champion recruits, chess masters and mathematicians — picked up the torch. Alan Turing, who had already defined the theoretical "universal machine" in 1936, designed an electro-mechanical monster to attack the daily Enigma settings: the Bombe, refined with Gordon Welchman's brilliant "diagonal board". The first Bombe, named Victory, started running in March 1940; dozens followed, clanking through the night.

Common confusion: the Bombe is not the famous "Turing machine." The Turing machine is a 1936 thought experiment — the mathematical definition of what computation even is. The Bombe is the real, room-sized machine Turing engineered to do a very specific job: defeat Enigma. And the later Colossus (1944) is a third thing entirely — built by Tommy Flowers to attack the Lorenz cipher, not Enigma.

How the Bombe thought

The Bombe inverted the problem. Instead of trying 1.59 × 10²⁰ settings head-on, it eliminated them:

  1. Take a crib — e.g., you know WETTERVORHERSAGE ("weather forecast") appears somewhere in today's messages.
  2. Slide the crib against the ciphertext at every possible offset. At the correct alignment, no ciphertext letter may equal its plaintext letter (the reflector flaw becomes a weapon).
  3. Model the logical consequences of that assumption as an electrical circuit across the rotors' 17,576 possible positions. Contradictions — a letter forced to encrypt to itself — kill whole families of settings instantly.
  4. The few surviving "stops" went to humans for hand-testing.

Settings that would take forever by brute force died in minutes by logic. The decrypted intelligence, codenamed Ultra, was handled with paranoid secrecy — Churchill reportedly never acted on it without considering whether the Germans would realize Enigma was compromised. Historian Harry Hinsley estimated Ultra shortened the war by around two years, saving millions of lives.

What came after

  • The first computers. Bombe thinking led directly to Colossus, the first large-scale electronic computer — and Turing's 1945–48 designs (ACE, and the Manchester Baby era) turned wartime machinery into general-purpose computers. Modern computing has Bletchley in its family tree.
  • Modern cryptography learned the lesson. Post-war ciphers assume the algorithm is public and all security lives in keys — that's exactly how today's AES and RSA are designed (and Kerckhoffs finally became law).
  • Alan Turing's tragedy. In 1952 Britain prosecuted him for being gay; he lost his security clearance and died in 1954. He received a royal pardon in 2013, and an official apology — far too late, from a country his machines saved.
  • The field was born. Cryptoanalysis, cybersecurity, and information security all trace their professional lineage to Bletchley's huts.

Try it yourself

snippet.python
# A Caesar cipher — Enigma's ancestor, 2000 years older
def caesar(text: str, shift: int) -> str:
    result = []
    for ch in text.upper():
        if ch.isalpha():
            result.append(chr((ord(ch) - 65 + shift) % 26 + 65))
        else:
            result.append(ch)
    return "".join(result)

print(caesar("ATTACK", 3))   # DWWDFN

Break this with frequency analysis, then imagine adding three rotating alphabets, a reflector, and a plugboard — and you understand why Enigma took the world's best minds six years of war to defeat.

Why this story matters

The Enigma story is the clearest proof that software is a weapon of mathematics. A war was fought twice: once on the battlefield, once in the world of pure logic — and the logic won. It's also the origin story of your profession: the computer you carry is a descendant of machines built to read secrets. Security by obscurity failed; key discipline and mathematical rigor won. Everything we call cybersecurity today started in those huts.

Historical figures and estimates follow the standard accounts (Hinsley, Bletchley Park Trust, Imperial War Museums); details were classified for decades, so minor numbers vary between sources.
