25:T35d3,<p>Every computer engineer should fall in love with one origin story, and
mine is this one: a war won not only by soldiers, but by mathematicians
attacking a machine — and in doing so, accidentally inventing modern
computing. The Enigma story has everything: a cipher that looked
mathematically invincible, a fatal design flaw, Polish heroes nobody
mentioned for decades, and Alan Turing building a machine to fight
another machine.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>TL;DR</strong> — Enigma was a German cipher machine with ~159 quintillion
daily settings. Its one mathematical flaw (a letter never encrypts to
itself) plus predictable operator habits let Turing&#39;s electro-mechanical
<strong>Bombe</strong> machines eliminate settings by the millions — and the decoded
&quot;Ultra&quot; intelligence is estimated to have shortened WWII by two years.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="cryptography-before-enigma-a-2000-year-arms-race">Cryptography before Enigma: a 2,000-year arms race</h2><p>Cryptography — hiding the <em>meaning</em> of a message — is older than the
alphabet itself. Two ideas dominated for two thousand years:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Substitution:</strong> shift or scramble letters. Julius Caesar shifted each
letter by 3: <code>ATTACK → DWWDFN</code>.</li>
<li><strong>Transposition:</strong> keep the letters, shuffle their order.</li>
</ul>
<p>The counter-attack was <strong>frequency analysis</strong>, perfected in the 9th
century by <strong>Al-Kindi</strong> in Baghdad: in any language some letters appear
more often, so a substitution cipher leaks its secrets through statistics.
The arms race then produced the <strong>Vigenère cipher</strong> (16th century), which
used a keyword to shift each letter differently — &quot;le chiffre
indéchiffrable&quot;, unbreakable, until Charles Babbage and Kasiski cracked it
too in the 19th century.</p>
<p>The lesson that shapes everything after: <strong>a cipher&#39;s security cannot
rest on secrecy of the method</strong> — attackers will know the method. (This
became Kerckhoffs&#39;s principle, 1883: assume the enemy knows the system;
security must live in the key alone.)</p>
<h2 id="the-enigma-machine">The Enigma machine</h2><p>In 1918, German engineer <strong>Arthur Scherbius</strong> patented a machine that
mechanized substitution so fast and so complex that frequency analysis
would drown: <strong>Enigma</strong>. The German military adopted and hardened it
through the 1920s and 30s.</p>
<h3 id="how-it-worked">How it worked</h3><p>Imagine a typewriter wired to an electrical maze:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Keyboard → rotors.</strong> Pressing a key sends current through three
rotating wheels (<strong>rotors</strong>), each rewiring the alphabet differently.</li>
<li><strong>The reflector.</strong> Current exits the third rotor into a fixed reflector
that sends it back through the rotors along a <em>different</em> path, lighting
up one lamp — that letter is your ciphertext.</li>
<li><strong>The plugboard.</strong> Before and after the rotors, pairs of letters could
be swapped with cables — adding enormous extra complexity.</li>
<li><strong>Rotation.</strong> After every keypress the right rotor steps (like an
odometer), so the next letter is encrypted with a completely different
wiring. <code>AAAAA</code> almost never comes out as <code>BBBBB</code>.</li>
</ol>
<p>Each operator set the machine each morning with a <strong>daily key</strong>: which
rotors (from a box of 3-out-of-5), their starting positions, ring
settings, and up to 10 plugboard pairs. The result:</p>
<div class="term" dir="ltr"><div class="term-bar"><span class="d d1"></span><span class="d d2"></span><span class="d d3"></span><span class="file">snippet.txt</span><button type="button" class="copy" data-copy>COPY</button></div><pre class="shiki vitesse-dark" style="background-color:#121212;color:#dbd7caee" tabindex="0"><code><span class="line"><span>158,962,555,217,826,360,000  ≈ 1.59 × 10²⁰ possible settings</span></span></code></pre></div><p>Fifteen-eight quintillion. Try one setting per second, and you&#39;d need
more time than the age of the universe. The Germans were, reasonably,
convinced it was unbreakable.</p>
<h3 id="the-fatal-flaws">The fatal flaws</h3><p>Enigma had three weaknesses — none obvious, all fatal:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>A letter can never encrypt to itself.</strong> The reflector guaranteed it.
Sounds harmless — it&#39;s a mathematical fingerprint.</li>
<li><strong>Humans are predictable.</strong> Germans sent predictable messages at
predictable times: weather reports (<code>WETTER</code>), routine reports, and
formal phrases like &quot;Heil Hitler&quot; — so codebreakers knew pieces of the
plaintext. Such guessed fragments are called <strong>cribs</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>The daily key wasn&#39;t the whole secret.</strong> Early procedures reused
message keys in structured ways, leaking patterns the mathematicians
could exploit.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="the-codebreakers">The codebreakers</h2><p>The first break wasn&#39;t British. In <strong>1932</strong>, Polish mathematician
<strong>Marian Rejewski</strong> — using reconstructive mathematics and intelligence
from French contacts — reconstructed Enigma&#39;s internal wiring <em>from pure
paper analysis</em>. With <strong>Jerzy Różycki</strong> and <strong>Henryk Zygalski</strong>, he built
tooling including a machine called the <strong>bomba</strong>. When Germany added two
more rotors in 1938, the Polish methods expired — so in <strong>July 1939</strong>, at
a secret meeting near Warsaw, the Poles handed everything to British and
French intelligence. Five weeks later, WWII began.</p>
<p>Bletchley Park, England — a country estate stuffed with
crossword-champion recruits, chess masters and mathematicians — picked up
the torch. <strong>Alan Turing</strong>, who had already defined the theoretical
&quot;universal machine&quot; in 1936, designed an electro-mechanical monster to
attack the daily Enigma settings: the <strong>Bombe</strong>, refined with Gordon
Welchman&#39;s brilliant &quot;diagonal board&quot;. The first Bombe, named <em>Victory</em>,
started running in March 1940; dozens followed, clanking through the
night.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Common confusion:</strong> the <strong>Bombe</strong> is not the famous &quot;Turing machine.&quot;
The Turing machine is a 1936 <em>thought experiment</em> — the mathematical
definition of what computation even is. The Bombe is the real,
room-sized machine Turing engineered to do a very specific job: defeat
Enigma. And the later <strong>Colossus</strong> (1944) is a third thing entirely —
built by Tommy Flowers to attack the Lorenz cipher, not Enigma.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="how-the-bombe-thought">How the Bombe thought</h2><p>The Bombe inverted the problem. Instead of trying 1.59 × 10²⁰ settings
head-on, it <strong>eliminated</strong> them:</p>
<ol>
<li>Take a <strong>crib</strong> — e.g., you know <code>WETTERVORHERSAGE</code> (&quot;weather
forecast&quot;) appears somewhere in today&#39;s messages.</li>
<li>Slide the crib against the ciphertext at every possible offset. At the
correct alignment, no ciphertext letter may equal its plaintext letter
(the reflector flaw becomes a weapon).</li>
<li>Model the logical consequences of that assumption as an electrical
circuit across the rotors&#39; 17,576 possible positions. Contradictions
— a letter forced to encrypt to itself — kill whole families of
settings instantly.</li>
<li>The few surviving &quot;stops&quot; went to humans for hand-testing.</li>
</ol>
<p>Settings that would take forever by brute force died in minutes by logic.
The decrypted intelligence, codenamed <strong>Ultra</strong>, was handled with
paranoid secrecy — Churchill reportedly never acted on it without
considering whether the Germans would realize Enigma was compromised.
Historian Harry Hinsley estimated Ultra <strong>shortened the war by around
two years</strong>, saving millions of lives.</p>
<h2 id="what-came-after">What came after</h2><ul>
<li><strong>The first computers.</strong> Bombe thinking led directly to Colossus, the
first large-scale electronic computer — and Turing&#39;s 1945–48 designs
(ACE, and the Manchester Baby era) turned wartime machinery into
general-purpose computers. Modern computing has Bletchley in its
family tree.</li>
<li><strong>Modern cryptography learned the lesson.</strong> Post-war ciphers assume
the algorithm is public and all security lives in keys — that&#39;s
exactly how today&#39;s <strong>AES</strong> and <strong>RSA</strong> are designed (and Kerckhoffs
finally became law).</li>
<li><strong>Alan Turing&#39;s tragedy.</strong> In 1952 Britain prosecuted him for being
gay; he lost his security clearance and died in 1954. He received a
royal pardon in 2013, and an official apology — far too late, from a
country his machines saved.</li>
<li><strong>The field was born.</strong> Cryptoanalysis, cybersecurity, and
information security all trace their professional lineage to
Bletchley&#39;s huts.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="why-this-story-matters">Why this story matters</h2><p>The Enigma story is the clearest proof that <strong>software is a weapon of
mathematics</strong>. A war was fought twice: once on the battlefield, once in
the world of pure logic — and the logic won. It&#39;s also the origin story
of your profession: the computer you carry is a descendant of machines
built to read secrets. Security by obscurity failed; key discipline and
mathematical rigor won. Everything we call cybersecurity today started
in those huts.</p>
